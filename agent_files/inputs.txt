\begin{abstract}
The stabilizing effect of a sheared axial flow is investigated in the ZaP flow Z-pinch experiment at the University of Washington. Long-lived, hydrogen Z-pinch plasmas are generated that are 1 m long with an approximately 10 mm radius and exhibit gross stability for many Alfvén transit times. Large magnetic fluctuations occur during pinch assembly, after which the amplitude and frequency of the fluctuations diminish. This stable behaviour continues for an extended quiescent period. At the end of the quiescent period, fluctuation levels increase in magnitude and frequency. Axial flow profiles are determined by measuring the Doppler shift of plasma impurity lines using a 20 -chord spectrometer. Experimental measurements show a sheared flow that is coincident with low magnetic fluctuations during the quiescent period. The experimental flow shear exceeds the theoretical threshold during the quiescent period, and the flow shear is lower than the theoretical threshold at other times. The observed plasma behaviour and correlation between the sheared flow and stability persists as the amount of injected neutral gas and experimental geometry are varied. Computer simulations using experimentally observed plasma profiles show a consistent sheared flow stabilization effect. Plasma pinch parameters are measured independently to demonstrate an equilibrium consistent with radial force balance.
\end{abstract}

\section*{1. Introduction}
The Z-pinch provides a simple magnetic confinement configuration for plasma that has many advantages both as a possible fusion reactor and as a test bed to conduct basic plasma science research. The equilibrium is described by the radial force balance


\begin{equation*}
\frac{B_{\theta}}{\mu_{o} r} \frac{\mathrm{~d}\left(r B_{\theta}\right)}{\mathrm{d} r}=-\frac{\mathrm{d} p}{\mathrm{~d} r} . \tag{1}
\end{equation*}


The pure Z -pinch is simply connected and has unity average beta. The only magnetic field present is the $B_{\theta}$ field generated by the plasma current. All magnetic field lines are closed, even though the plasma has open ends. However, the pure Z-pinch is classically unstable to the $m=0$ sausage and $m=1$ kink modes.

Conventional techniques to provide stability for the Z-pinch have drawbacks. The sausage mode can be stabilized if the pressure gradient is limited [1]. Controlling the pressure profile is difficult and the technique does not stabilize the kink\\[0pt]
mode. An axial magnetic field can be applied to provide stability. However, the plasma current and the pressure are limited by the strength of the axial magnetic field according to the Kruskal-Shafranov limit [2,3]. Furthermore, an axial magnetic field opens all field lines and connects the electrodes to all regions of the plasma. A close-fitting, conducting wall can provide stability if the wall is located close to the plasma edge, $r_{\text {wall }} / a=1.2$ [4], which is incompatible with a hot, fusion-grade plasma. The influence of an axial magnetic field and a conducting wall on the stability of a Z-pinch is also investigated in a classical work [5], which also reveals a stabilizing effect when the conducting wall approaches the plasma radius.

Flow shear can stabilize the MHD modes in a pure Z-pinch without the drawbacks of the conventional stabilization techniques. An axial flow does not alter the radial force balance that describes a Z-pinch equilibrium, see (1). Since the plasma geometry is simple, i.e. described by a one-dimensional force balance, the Z-pinch configuration is ideal for studying stability characteristics and isolating flow shear stabilization physics.

Linear stability analysis demonstrates a stabilizing effect of a sheared axial flow on the $m=1$ kink instability in Z-pinches when the shear exceeds a threshold value, $\mathrm{d} V_{z} / \mathrm{d} r> 0.1 k V_{\mathrm{A}}$ [4], assuming a uniform shear throughout the plasma. Other investigations have found that large values of the flow speed (Mach 2) are required for stability [6]. The investigations use different equilibrium velocity profiles. Reference [4] assumed a uniform shear throughout the plasma except at the axis and [6] assumed a shear that increases linearly from zero at the axis $[7,8]$.

Previous experiments have generated Z -pinch plasmas that exist for times longer than theoretically predicted by static plasma theory $[9,10]$. These experiments have generated Z-pinch plasmas which inherently contain an axial plasma flow.

\section*{2. ZaP flow Z-pinch experiment}
Experiments are conducted with the ZaP flow Z -pinch experiment at the University of Washington to investigate using sheared flows to provide stability in a pure Z-pinch. As described in [11], the ZaP flow Z-pinch experiment initiates a hydrogen plasma with a 1 m coaxial accelerator that has a 0.2 m diameter outer electrode and a 0.1 m diameter inner electrode. Neutral gas is injected through puff valves into the acceleration region. The plasma is accelerated to a large axial velocity by a Lorentz force, exits the accelerator and forms a Z-pinch plasma 1 m in length and approximately 10 mm in radius. Current in the accelerator continues to accelerate plasma into the Z-pinch assembly replacing plasma as it exits the Z-pinch. Inertia maintains the axial flow within the Zpinch plasma column. A 10 mm radius hole is located along the axis in the electrode end wall to allow the axial plasma flow to escape from the assembly region and into a surge volume of the vacuum vessel. A machine drawing of the experiment is shown in figure 1, which identifies the relevant hardware features.

Experimental results reported here also include results from the recently modified ZaP experiment. The inner\\
electrode of the coaxial accelerator has been replaced with a larger diameter electrode, approximately 0.15 m . The new inner electrode also has added capacity and control for neutral gas injection.

\section*{3. Plasma fluctuations and velocity profile measurements}
Diagnostics on the ZaP experiment are designed to measure the plasma flow profile and the stability of the pinch, as well as the plasma equilibrium parameters. Plasma stability is diagnosed with an azimuthal array of eight magnetic probes that measures the plasma's magnetic structure. The magnetic field probes of the azimuthal array are embedded in the inner surface of the outer electrode at the ' $z=0$ ' location shown in figure 1 . The eight probes are uniformly distributed azimuthally ( $45^{\circ}$ apart). Data from these probes are Fourier analysed to determine the time-dependent evolution of the low-order azimuthal modes ( $m=1,2,3$ ), as shown in figure 2. These probes also provide a measure of the average magnetic field ( $B_{0}$ ) and the corresponding axial current through the plasma column. The Fourier components of the magnetic field are normalized by the average magnetic field. The current sheet passes $z=0$ at $22 \mu \mathrm{~s}$, where the normalized magnetic fluctuation data begin in figure 2. Before this time, $B_{0}=0$ and the normalized data have no meaning. Large magnetic fluctuations occur during pinch assembly, after which the amplitude and frequency of the magnetic fluctuations diminish. This stable behaviour continues for $35-45 \mu \mathrm{~s}$. The quiescent period is defined when the normalized $m=1$ fluctuation levels are sustained below a threshold of 0.2 , which corresponds to a 1 cm displacement of the plasma current from the geometric axis. A $37 \mu$ s quiescent period is seen in figure 2. Fluctuations during assembly generally reduce the highest $m$ first, as shown in figure 2 . Fluctuations are likely due to the dynamics of the formation process. At the end of the quiescent period, the fluctuation levels again change character, increase in magnitude and frequency and remain until the end of the plasma pulse.

The chordintegrated data are deconvolved using a shell/matrix method to give the radial profiles of the axial velocity [12]. Only one exposure of the ICCD camera is possible during a plasma pulse.

\section*{4. Z-pinch simulations with nonuniform flow shear}
The flow velocity profiles experimentally measured throughout the quiescent period generally do not show a uniform flow shear across the pinch as assumed in the linear stability analysis. Instead, the velocity profiles typically show a flow shear that is localized at the edge of the pinch radius ( $a=10 \mathrm{~mm}$ ).

To test the effect of nonuniform flow shear on stability, nonlinear computer simulations of the $m=0$ mode in a Z-pinch plasma are performed using Mach2 [16], a time-dependent, resistive MHD code. Three-dimensional simulations of additional modes ( $m=1,2,3$ ) would allow a more complete comparison with the experimental measurements and are the subject of a future study. An equilibrium is initialized with a parabolic pressure profile and an axially periodic density perturbation. A series of simulations are performed with varying plasma flow shear. \section*{5. Experimental equilibrium measurements}
The theoretical threshold for the sheared flow stabilization is computed based on measurements of the plasma equilibrium using plasma density from interferometry, pinch radius from interferometry, and magnetic field extrapolated from magnetic probes in the outer electrode. The plasma electron and ion temperatures ( $T_{\mathrm{e}}$ and $T_{\mathrm{i}}$ ) are measured independently to check for agreement with the plasma parameters by using equilibrium force balance, (1). The equilibrium force balance can be expressed as


\begin{equation*}
\frac{B_{\theta}}{\mu_{o} r} \frac{\mathrm{~d}\left(r B_{\theta}\right)}{\mathrm{d} r}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{~d} r}\left[n k\left(T_{\mathrm{e}}+T_{\mathrm{i}}\right)\right] \tag{2}
\end{equation*}


for a charge-neutral ( $n=n_{\mathrm{e}}=n_{\mathrm{i}}$ ) hydrogen plasma, where $k$ is Boltzmann's constant.

The electron temperature is measured with a single-point Thomson scattering system. The diagnostic uses a 10 J Korad ruby laser which crosses the pinch plasma at $z=0$ along a diameter using the horizontal viewports shown in figure 1. Scattered light is collected by fibres on the top viewport. The light collection system views a cylindrical scattering volume 2.5 mm long and 3 mm diameter at the machine axis along the focused laser beam waist. The scattered light is directed to a custom-built Hibshman Thomson scattering spectrometer which uses line filters to remove the parasitic ruby light ( 694 nm ) and the $\mathrm{H}_{\alpha}$ emission. The output field of the spectrometer is recorded with an array of eight photomultiplier tubes (PMTs) distributed across the scattered spectrum. The laser pulse and scatter pulse waveforms are recorded, as well as the background light before and after the scatter pulse. A representative scattered spectrum is shown in figure 12. The spectrum is fitted with a Gaussian line profile corresponding to a 64 eV electron temperature.

The ion temperature is determined by measuring the Doppler broadening of impurity lines caused by thermal motion. The same ICCD spectrometer that is used to make velocity profile measurements is also used to measure the ion temperature by viewing the plasma through a radial viewport. For these data, the bottom viewport at $z=0$ in figure 1 is used to measure the broadened spectrum of the C III impurity line at 229.7 nm along a chord through the plasma diameter. The spectrum shown in figure 13 shows the experimentally measured spectrum and a numerical fit of a Gaussian line profile corresponding to a 71 eV ion temperature. The spectrum is recorded early during the quiescent period during a 100 ns gate.

During the same gate time used for the ICCD, data from a four-chord interferometer through the pinch plasma are analysed. The four-chord interferometer is an expanded version of the two-chord interferometer used on the original geometry [13]. Assuming a uniform density inside the pinch surrounded by a uniform background density gives a pinch density of $n_{\mathrm{e}}=1.5 \times 10^{22} \mathrm{~m}^{-3}$ and a background, surrounding density of $n_{\mathrm{e}}=2 \times 10^{21} \mathrm{~m}^{-3}$ with a pinch radius $a=13 \mathrm{~mm}$. During this time, the magnetic field at the outer electrode is measured at 0.13 T . If no current flows outside of the pinch, the magnetic field at the pinch radius is $B_{a}=1 \mathrm{~T}$. For a uniform pressure inside the pinch, the equilibrium force balance gives a total temperature $T_{\mathrm{e}}+T_{\mathrm{i}}=160 \mathrm{eV}$. This value can be compared with the independently measured electron and ion temperatures $T_{\mathrm{e}}+T_{\mathrm{i}}=135 \mathrm{eV}$. The comparison suggests that the equilibrium values used to compute the theoretical shear threshold are valid.

\section*{6. Conclusions}
The ZaP project is producing Z-pinch plasmas that exhibit gross stability during an extended quiescent period, consistent with flow stabilization theory. The quiescent period is coincident with a sheared plasma flow. The magnitude of the sheared plasma flow is consistent with flow stabilization theory, even for different amounts of injected neutral gas. Neutral gas supply appears to limit the plasma lifetime and suggests a technique to produce long-lived plasmas. The behaviour remains consistent for the different amounts of injected neutral gas and as the diameter of the inner electrode of the coaxial accelerator is increased by $50 \%$. Nonlinear computer simulations demonstrate stabilization with flow profiles that have a shear localized at the plasma edge, similar to experimental measurements. A flow-stabilized Z-pinch has many important implications for a simple reactor design with average unity $\beta$ and a simply connected geometry. In addition, the stabilizing effect of sheared flows has important applications to other magnetic confinement concepts. Understanding the physical effects of sheared flows on stability is important to advancing our general understanding of plasma physics and specifically magnetic fusion science, beyond the flow Z-pinch concept alone.
